# The Slop Test

Run every draft through this before showing it to the owner. These are the specific patterns that make copy read as machine-written, and owners recognize all of them instantly because their own inboxes are full of them.

---

## The four fast checks

**1. Would the owner say this sentence out loud?**

Read it aloud in their voice. If it sounds like a brochure, rewrite it. Business email that nobody would ever say is the most common tell there is.

**2. Could this go to a different company with two words changed?**

If yes, it is a template with a merge field, not personalization. The prospect has received forty of these this month.

**3. Does it open with a compliment about their business?**

Delete it. "I've been following your work and I'm impressed by what you're building" is the single most recognizable opener in cold email. Everyone uses it, so it signals mass send.

**4. Is there a phrase here the owner has never used?**

Check against the never-uses list in the shared voice profile. One foreign phrase undoes an otherwise good message.

---

## Banned openers

These read as generated regardless of what follows.

- "I hope this email finds you well"
- "I hope you're doing well"
- "I wanted to reach out"
- "I came across your company and"
- "I've been following your work"
- "Quick question for you"
- "I'll keep this brief"
- "I know you're busy, so"

**What to open with instead:** the specific reason you are writing. The permit filing. The mutual customer. The thing that happened.

```
Bad:  I hope this finds you well. I wanted to reach out because I came across
      Ridgeline and was impressed by your portfolio.

Good: Saw you filed for the Kellogg Street building. Congratulations.
```

---

## Banned filler

Phrases that occupy space without carrying meaning.

| Cut | Because |
|---|---|
| "reach out" / "circle back" / "touch base" | Corporate register almost no owner actually uses |
| "leverage" / "utilize" / "solutions" | Nobody says these out loud |
| "at your earliest convenience" | Say "when you get a chance" |
| "I'd love to" | Usually means "I want to" — say that |
| "excited to" | Manufactured enthusiasm reads as fake |
| "synergy" / "align" / "unlock" | Instantly recognizable as generated |
| "just following up" / "just bumping this" | Says the follow-up has no content |
| "let me know if you have any questions" | Filler closer; use a real ask |

---

## Structural tells

**Three neat parallel bullets.** Real people write one thing, or a messy list. Perfectly balanced trios are a generation artifact.

**A sentence that restates the previous one.** Generated copy pads. Owners do not.

**Every paragraph the same length.** Real writing is lumpy.

**Em dashes everywhere.** Owners use commas and periods. A message with three em dashes was not written by a contractor between jobs.

**A closing that summarizes what was just said.** In a 90-word email, this is filler by definition.

---

## The ask

Every message needs exactly one, and it should be small.

```
Bad:  Let me know if you'd like to explore how we might be able to help
      with your facilities needs.

Good: Worth a 10-minute call Thursday?
```

A vague ask gets a vague non-response. A specific small ask gets a yes or a no, and both are useful.

---

## Length

| Message | Ceiling |
|---|---|
| Cold, first touch | 120 words |
| Follow-up | 60 words |
| Warm or referral | 150 words |
| Post-meeting | 100 words |

Over the ceiling, cut rather than tighten. The parts that go first are the opener, the credentials paragraph, and the closing summary — none of them earn their space.

## Worked rewrite

**Before — 118 words, fails every check:**

```
Hi Dana,

I hope this email finds you well! I wanted to reach out because I came across
Ridgeline Property Group and was really impressed by the portfolio you've built
in the commercial space.

At Okonkwo Mechanical, we specialize in providing comprehensive HVAC solutions
tailored to the unique needs of multi-building property managers. We'd love to
explore how we might be able to leverage our expertise to help optimize your
facilities operations.

Would you be open to a brief call to discuss how we can add value to your
portfolio? Let me know if you have any questions!

Best regards,
Ray Okonkwo
```

**After — 54 words, in Ray's actual voice:**

```
Hi Dana,

Saw you filed for the Kellogg Street building. Congratulations.

Straight answer on why I'm writing: we cover six other property managers in
the area on single service plans across all their buildings. Usually works out
cheaper than per-building contracts.

Worth 10 minutes Thursday?

Thanks, Ray
```

What changed: the opener became a real fact. The credentials became a specific claim about comparable customers. The ask became answerable. Every banned phrase is gone. And it sounds like Ray, because it uses his greeting, his sign-off, his "straight answer," and his sentence length.

---

## When a draft cannot pass

Sometimes there is genuinely no hook — no signal, no connection, nothing specific.

Do not manufacture one. Write a shorter, honestly cold message and tell the owner it is cold:

```
No specific trigger on this one, so it's a straight cold intro. Shorter on
purpose — there's nothing to reference and pretending otherwise is worse.
```

An honest cold email at 40 words outperforms a fake-warm one at 120, and it does not cost the owner credibility if the prospect ever compares notes.
